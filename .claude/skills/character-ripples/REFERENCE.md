# Character Ripples — Reference Guide

## Core Premise

Every action a character takes sends ripples through the room. Other people react: physically, emotionally, socially. When they don't, the world feels frozen — characters become props, scenes become monologues dressed as dialogue, and the fiction loses the sense that anything is actually happening.

The ripple pass is an editing technique for identifying and repairing these frozen moments.

---

## The Four Failure Modes

### 1. Frozen Baseline
The room is already still before anything happens. Background characters have no micro-behavior, no ongoing lives. The scene opens with only the POV character and their target; everyone else is furniture.

**Signs:**
- No ambient behavior before the inciting action
- Secondary characters introduced only when needed, then forgotten
- The setting feels like a painted backdrop

### 2. No Consequence
A character does something significant — raises their voice, draws a weapon, breaks a rule, makes a confession — and the people nearby show no response. The action lands in a vacuum.

**Signs:**
- Dialogue continues without physical acknowledgment
- Body language of bystanders never updates after major beats
- Power moves happen without social fall-out

### 3. Prop Characters
Named or unnamed characters are present but function only as set dressing. They exist to deliver a line or confirm a beat, then disappear back into scenery. They have no stake in what's happening.

**Signs:**
- A character speaks once and is never referenced again
- The same bystander "watches" every scene without ever having a specific reaction
- Characters described by function only ("the guard", "a servant") with no individuation

### 4. No Management
The POV character is so locked into their own emotional state that they stop perceiving the room. The narrative follows the interiority tunnel and loses track of the external world entirely. The POV fails to manage — track, read, influence — the people around them.

**Signs:**
- Long internal spirals with no sensory anchors
- POV character doesn't register other people's reactions at key moments
- The POV's emotional state is described in abstract terms disconnected from concrete stimuli

## Flag System

Place flags inline, at the sentence or paragraph where the failure occurs. Follow each flag with a parenthetical rationale.

| Flag | Usage |
|------|-------|
| `[FROZEN ROOM]` | Scene opens with no ambient life; baseline is static |
| `[NO RIPPLE: type — who]` | Action of `type` happened but `who` didn't respond |
| `[PROP CHARACTER: Name]` | Name/role is physically present but has no stake or reaction |
| `[NO MANAGEMENT: POV — stake]` | POV character lost the room; missed reading `stake` |
| `[CAUSALITY GAP]` | A reaction is shown but the trigger is absent or reversed in order |
| `[STILLNESS HOLDS]` | An earlier freeze was flagged but has persisted through the next beat |
| `[TUNNEL VISION HOLDS]` | POV's interiority spiral continues past the point of sensory re-entry |

**Flag format:**
```
[FLAG TYPE: specifics]
(Rationale: what should be here, who should react, what the ripple would look like)
```

## Social Geometry

When choreographing ripples, consider four axes:

### Status Protocol
Who defers to whom? Does a low-status character flinch, avoid eye contact, step back when a high-status character moves? Does a high-status character fail to acknowledge a low-status one — and does the low-status character register that non-acknowledgment?

### Witness Composition
Who is watching and what is their relationship to the action? A stranger reads differently from a friend, an enemy, a neutral third party. The same action witnessed by a loyalist vs. a rival produces different ripple shapes.

### Spatial Position
Where are bodies in the room? Who has to step around whom? Who is between the two principals and therefore caught in the blast radius? Who has a wall at their back vs. an exit route?

### Power Asymmetry
When the power differential between characters shifts mid-scene, the room's posture should shift with it. A character who was relaxed should tighten. A character who was tense may relax or may escalate depending on which side of the shift they land on.

## The Ripple Pass — Process

### Step 1: Inventory
Before flagging, note for each scene:
- **Position**: Where is everyone? (list by name or role)
- **Presence**: Who has been individuated vs. who is scenery?
- **Ambient**: What is the baseline behavior before the inciting action?

### Step 2: Walk the Actions
For each significant action (any action that changes status, reveals information, threatens, offers, or refuses):
- Who is in range to perceive it?
- What would a realistic person in their position feel?
- What would they do with that feeling in the next 2–5 seconds?

### Step 3: Place Flags Inline
Annotate the manuscript at the sentence level. Do not rewrite yet — flag only.

### Step 4: Rationale Block
After each flag, write a one- to three-sentence rationale explaining:
1. What the missing ripple is
2. Who should produce it
3. What form it would take (gesture, word, exit, stillness that reads as deliberate)

### Step 5: Repair Suggestions (optional)
If the ripple is mechanical to add, offer a draft line. If it requires restructuring (e.g., a prop character needs to be established earlier to have a meaningful reaction), note the scope.

## What a Good Ripple Looks Like

Good ripples are:
- **Specific to the character**: Not "someone shifted uncomfortably" but "Maren set down her glass without drinking from it."
- **Proportional to the action**: A murmur doesn't clear the room; a drawn blade does.
- **Temporally accurate**: Ripples happen in real time, not after the scene processes the event. The flinch comes before the thought.
- **Calibrated by distance and stake**: The person standing next to the action ripples more than the person across the room. The person with the most to lose ripples most.

---

## Common Mistakes in Repair

- **Over-rippling**: Every beat gets three reactions; the scene becomes a catalog of micro-behavior and loses momentum. Ripple the beats that matter; let minor beats breathe.
- **Symmetric rippling**: Every character reacts the same way (everyone goes quiet, everyone tenses). Real rooms are asymmetric — one person leans in while another leans away.
- **Narrated ripple instead of shown**: "The room felt the tension" is not a ripple. Ripples are concrete actions in specific bodies.
- **Ripple interrupts POV logic**: Adding a ripple that the POV character couldn't have noticed (they have their back turned, they're deep in internal monologue) breaks point of view.
